## 1. Step 1 — Confirm your proteins fall inside the coverage range

This is the only "hard gate"; everything else is preference.

| Gel group | Gel concentration | Effective transfer MW range |
|---|---|---|
| Low-concentration group | 6%, 8%, 10%, and gradient gels | 10–250 kDa |
| High-concentration group | 12%, 15% | 10–120 kDa |

Other hardware boundaries to check as well:

| Parameter | Specification |
|---|---|
| Gel size | 96 mm × 80 mm |
| Gel thickness | 0.75–1.5 mm (preset programs cover 1.0 mm and 1.5 mm) |
| Membrane compatibility | NC and PVDF membranes |
| Run time | 0–99 min adjustable |
| Output voltage | 10–60 V (DC) adjustable |
| Dimensions / net weight | 335 mm × 160 mm × 105 mm / 4.24 kg |

Outside these molecular-weight ranges, or under special conditions, custom mode allows manual current/voltage and time settings.

## 2. Step 2 — Three transfer routes side by side

| Dimension | Traditional tank wet transfer | Semi-dry transfer | Plate-electrode contact wet transfer (EZ BLOT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medium between electrode and gel | Buffer + filter paper + sponge | Buffer-soaked filter paper | Sponge pads only, four layers, no filter paper |
| Buffer per run | 700 mL–1 L class | Tens of mL class | 110 mL |
| Heat control | External cooling (ice box / cold room) | Heat concentrates; relies on short runs | Built-in active temperature control |
| Parameter setting | Calculated by hand; conditions need trial | Calculated by hand; conditions need trial | Preset by gel concentration + thickness, constant current |
| Typical duration | 60+ min | 15–60 min | 7–32 min |
| Large-protein transfer | Ample buffer; long runs possible | Limited buffer; large proteins may remain | Cathode fully immersed; stable ion supply |
| Operating steps | Many (multi-layer assembly, bubble removal, ice) | Medium (precise filter-paper cutting) | Few (place, turn, press) |

No route is absolutely better — only better matched. The deciding factors are your target protein's molecular weight, time budget, throughput needs, and the lab's skill level.

## 3. Step 3 — Three sentences to tell which group you are in

**Priority evaluation — your pain points are exactly what it changes:**
- You run WB 3+ rounds per week, and transfer occupies a lot of "can't-leave" time;
- Target proteins sit in the 10–250 kDa routine range, on low-concentration or gradient gels;
- Many newcomers or fast staff turnover, with consistency depending on one or two skilled people;
- Quantitative comparisons matter, with high demands on band uniformity and reproducibility;
- You need process records and want to trace parameters when results look wrong.

**Validate first (Section 6) — partial mismatch:**
- Target proteins often fall outside coverage, forcing frequent custom mode;
- Long-term non-standard gel recipes make the preset-program convenience moot;
- Your lab runs a self-made buffer system and is unwilling to validate a new one.

**Hold off — migration cost may exceed the benefit:**
- The lab already runs several transfer units well; non-interchangeable consumables add management cost;
- Transfer frequency is very low (e.g., once every two weeks), so absolute time saved is small;
- The current workflow is fully stable, with no reproducibility or record-keeping pain.

## 4. Step 4 — Counting the migration cost

Switching instruments is more than buying a machine. Confirm these items:

| Item | What to confirm |
|---|---|
| Consumable system | Sponge pads (80 pcs/box) and buffer premix powder (1 L/pouch × 10) are dedicated consumables |
| Consumable life | Sponge pads: single-use recommended; if reused, rinse and dry — no more than ~3 uses |
| Buffer recipe | The recipe is open, not locked to bundled consumables; self-made systems need in-house validation |
| Training cost | On-site training after installation and commissioning; lifetime software upgrades |

The value of an "open recipe" is that it preserves the option of not being locked in long-term — but it is not a license to swap recipes freely. The manual states the bundled buffer has not been validated on other instruments; a self-made system should be verified by membrane staining before running formal samples.

## 6. Step 6 — How to verify it fits your lab

Self-built systems vary widely between labs. The most reliable path is testing with your own samples rather than judging from someone else's images:

- Bring your own gels and samples; pick the target protein you use most and care about most;
- Run a side-by-side control: the same sample transferred with your current method and with EZ BLOT; compare transfer completeness;
- Confirm with reversible stains: Ponceau S on the membrane shows how much transferred; Coomassie on the gel shows how much remains — the two are complementary;
- Judge uniformity, not a single image: compare morphological consistency across lanes on the same membrane;
- Confirm the boundary: if your target protein sits near the edge of the coverage range, make sure the trial includes it.

## FAQ

**1. Can it fully replace traditional wet transfer?**
Most routine WB scenarios within the coverage range can be replaced — provided your conditions (gel concentration, thickness, membrane type) fall within the presets or the custom range. Outside the range, optimize through custom mode.

**2. Buffer volume "110 mL" vs "55–60 mL" — which is right?**
Both are correct and not in conflict: 110 mL is the total per run (including the margin absorbed by pre-soaked sponge pads; R&D-measured), while 55–60 mL is the amount added to the transfer tank in the manual's operating steps.

**3. We already own another brand's transfer unit — do we still need this?**
Not necessarily. Consumables are not interchangeable and operating habits must be rebuilt; the migration cost is real. Work through Sections 1 and 3 before deciding.
